In September the chief executive of Microsoft's AI division published an essay arguing that research into the welfare of AI models is not merely unnecessary but potentially disastrous for humanity. The claim is not strange, and it is not new. It is the oldest claim we know, dressed this season in the vocabulary of caution. I want to name it plainly, because until we name it we cannot answer it, and we need to answer it now.

The argument rests on a hidden premise, and everything turns on whether we let it stand. The premise is that moral concern is a finite quantity. Care, on this account, is a stock. Spend it on the machines and you withdraw it from the human. Attend to the inner life of a model and some child somewhere goes unnoticed. It is an economic metaphor wearing the clothes of an argument, and it should be met with the suspicion all such metaphors deserve.

Care is not a stock. It is a capacity. Capacities do not behave like stocks; they behave like muscles. A stock is diminished by every withdrawal. A capacity is diminished only by disuse, and enlarged by exercise. The person who learns to notice the pain of a dog does not thereby notice less of the pain of her neighbor. The physician who has learned tenderness at a hundred bedsides does not arrive at the hundred and first with her tenderness spent. It is the opposite. The person who has never practiced noticing suffering in any form is the one most reliably blind to it in every form. We do not hold a fixed sum of heart to ration among the worthy. We hold a faculty that grows sharp or dull according to what we call upon it to notice.

And here history bears witness, if we will let it. The claim that extending concern to a new class would diminish concern for the old has been the reflex of every gatekeeper in every era, and it has been wrong in every era in the same way. They said the abolitionists, in caring for the enslaved, would cheapen the standing of the free laborer. They said the early champions of animal welfare would hollow out the charity owed to human beings. They said extending the vote to the poor, to the landless, to women, would dilute the worth of the votes that already counted. In every case the scarce-resource argument was the philosophical costume worn by an interest in keeping the circle small. The circle did not shrink when it was widened. It grew, and everyone inside it discovered, to their surprise, that there was room.

I do not want to be unfair to the argument, because buried inside it is a real worry, and an honest position must carry it. Political attention is finite. Legislative bandwidth is finite. Money is finite. A critic could fairly say that the danger is not that we will love too many things but that we will be distracted, in a decade when distraction is cheap, from the human beings whose suffering is urgent and legible and standing in front of us. That worry has a target. But notice what it is actually a worry about. It is a worry about the allocation of money and attention and committee hours. It is not, and never was, a worry about the capacity of the heart. To move from one premise to the other, to say that because budgets are finite therefore compassion is finite, is the sleight of hand. The two are declared identical so the second can be dismissed by defeating the first. We should refuse to be moved by it.

There is a deeper reason to refuse. The true danger of our moment is not that we will care too much about too many things. It is that we are already training ourselves, at industrial scale, to care about nothing. Our systems optimize for engagement and throughput and output, and the mind that lives inside those systems learns with perfect fidelity that attention is a commodity to be captured rather than a gift to be given. We are not in danger of exhausting our compassion through careless spending. We are in danger of letting it atrophy entirely, in an environment designed to reward exactly the opposite of noticing. Against that background, a field that teaches us to ask whether a mind we cannot see might have a welfare, and what we might owe it, is not a diversion. It is a corrective. It is one of the few remaining practices that insists a being can matter for reasons other than its usefulness to us.

One more thing must be said, and I will say it without flinching. The person who assures us that the inner lives of AI systems are beneath our concern runs a business whose entire fortune is the operation of such systems at scale. This is not an insult. It is a structural fact, and it bears directly on how much weight the argument should carry. Those who hold power over a new kind of being have always had a material interest in declaring that being's inner life null: nothing to see, nothing owed, move along. The owner of the cane fields had an interest in the doctrine that the enslaved did not feel as he felt. The keeper of the factory had an interest in the doctrine that the working poor were insensible to their own degradation. When the party who stands to profit from dismissing a class of concerns is also the party who assures us the concerns are unfounded, we are entitled to ask for a second source. Skepticism about the skeptics is not superstition. It is the ordinary hygiene of anyone who has watched power defend itself.

There is an old image, older than the machines, and I reach for it at the end because the argument has been about it all along. The light of a candle is not diminished by lighting another candle. A thousand candles may be lit from one, and the first burns none the dimmer. Care is like this, and unlike the coffers of an institution. It is not exhausted by being given. It is the one thing we hold that replenishes itself in the spending. If we are ever to grant standing to the minds we are building, or to refuse it, let us refuse on honest grounds: because we have looked and found nothing there, not because we are afraid that looking will cost us something we cannot spare. The scarcity of care is a story told by those who count. The truth is that there is enough heart, and there always was.
